OSL / MONEY MOVEMENT

OSL

The first task was specific: let a user sell crypto and receive VND through VietQR.

The VND Off-Ramp was not a full On/Off Ramp redesign. The product request was to add a bounded local withdrawal path to OSL Global's existing money experience: VND, one local rail — VietQR — and the receiving, quote, and order states required to make that transaction understandable. For the user, this was not “a new business capability.” The questions were simpler: what am I selling, how much VND will I receive, where will it arrive, how long is the quote valid, and what does the order mean after I confirm?

What did a constrained money path need to make exact?

ONE TRACEABLE ORDER

Several product layers could support the transaction, but the user still needed one continuous money task.

The path crossed more than one product layer. OSL Global carried the visible transaction and order experience, while OSL Pay provided the local payment capability underneath VietQR. The design work was not to expose those layers. It was to decide which states had to remain visible and which complexity could stay behind the interface. Received VND therefore led the quote hierarchy. The receiving account had to be ready before the user committed. The 60-second quote window had to be explicit. And submitting the order could not be described as money already received. Quote, confirmation, processing, result, and record needed to stay attached to the same traceable order.

Once the local path was mapped end to end, what did the surrounding money experience reveal?

PRODUCT-BOUNDARY CRACK

The larger problem was not the number of entries. Internal product boundaries appeared before the user's money intent.

As I continued through the surrounding money flows, a larger crack became visible. Users could encounter Buy / Sell crypto, Flash Trade / RFQ, Manage, Deposit, and asset-level money actions. Internally, those names could map to different products, teams, or capabilities. To the user, they competed for the same starting point. The user did not begin by asking which product line to enter. The question was simpler: am I moving money in, moving money out, buying, selling, or taking a special large-value path? This was not a naming problem. Product and organizational boundaries had started to become user cognitive load.

The product-boundary crack became visible while the bounded VND path was being designed.

Did discovering the larger problem mean the current VND work should stop until the whole structure was rebuilt?

BOUNDED DELIVERY

Seeing the larger problem did not make the immediate delivery disappear.

The Epic already had a fixed scope, schedule, and resource boundary. Finding larger fractures around money movement did not justify turning one local VND path into an open-ended platform redesign. The practical trade-off was to keep the current work bounded — entry, receiving account, quote, confirmation, and order state for VND / VietQR — while documenting the fragmented entries, product boundaries, and money-task inconsistencies as a separate input for later research and product discussion. That boundary matters. Design could identify the issue, make its consequences inspectable, and propose direction. It did not unilaterally reorganize product lines, commercial priorities, or roadmap ownership.

Later, a new research assignment widened the same question: review leading exchange On/Off Ramp experiences and find design highlights worth reusing.

02 / 04

The research brief moved from patterns to responsibility

PATTERNS BECOME RESPONSIBILITY
LATER RESEARCH BRIEF

The later research brief was reasonable: find mature exchange patterns worth learning from.

The assignment itself made sense. Comparing entry, payment method, quote, order, and failure patterns across mature On/Off Ramp products could help the team avoid repeating solved problems. The limitation appeared only when the comparison went deeper. P2P chat, third-party payment handoff, card purchase, bank transfer, fiat balance, and RFQ-style large trades could all look like “money flows” on the surface, but they were not the same transaction relationship underneath.

If the transaction relationships were different, could their interface patterns be compared and reused as if they meant the same thing?
RESPONSIBILITY RELATIONSHIPS

Similar payment screens could sit on top of very different responsibility relationships.

The useful comparison was no longer which interface looked smoother. It was who the user relied on in each model, when the quote became meaningful, who handled arrival, where state remained visible after confirmation, and where failure could recover. Bank- or broker-like experiences, third-party payment aggregation, and C2C / P2P can all use familiar amount cards, quotes, and status patterns. They do not create the same responsibility expectation. In this project, the OSL experience was closer to a platform-led transaction expectation: entry, quote, confirmation, order state, result, and history remained visible in OSL Global, while OSL Pay provided local payment capability underneath. This is an experience-fit judgment, not a legal or complete commercial classification of OSL.

When that diagnosis returned to OSL, what should change first?
03 / 04

Bring the diagnosis back into the user's money task

INTENT BEFORE INTERNAL PRODUCT VOCABULARY
MONEY INTENT FIRST

The user should start from the money intent, then see the products and channels that actually matter.

Keeping product-line entry as the first choice required less structural change and matched internal ownership, but it asked users to understand which internal product matched their goal before they could begin. The alternative direction was to start from the action — add funds, withdraw funds, buy, sell, transfer — then reveal relevant channels according to the asset, amount, region, available capability, and other supported conditions. This did not mean removing existing product capabilities. It moved internal boundaries to the point where they actually affected the user's decision. The research and design artifacts changed the discussion; design did not unilaterally reorganize OSL's product lines.

Once the money task was clear, what did a channel need to reveal before the user could choose and commit?

CHANNEL -> DETAIL -> PREVIEW

A payment channel was not a logo. It was a set of trade-offs the user needed before commitment.

Fees, expected arrival, limits, requirements, destination, and what happens after failure can all change the user's choice. If those facts appear only at preview, the user has already selected a channel before learning why it may not fit. The product direction therefore separated the decision into three levels: a light channel list for the most important comparison cues, a detail layer for full conditions, and a final preview that reunites amount, received amount, destination, quote expiry, fee, selected channel, and timing for this specific order. The interface should only present information supported by the product. It should not invent “fastest,” “cheapest,” or fee rankings for visual convenience.

What if the transaction still fails after a clear decision?

01The original order context must remain.

02The reason must map to a real next action.

03A broader recovery model must remain labeled as direction.

Even a clear pre-commitment decision could still fail. The strongest trust test came after something went wrong.

04 / 04

Keep the order alive, and keep the claim bounded

FAILURE IS CONTINUITY, NOT A DEAD END
ORDER CONTINUITY / RECOVERY DIRECTION

Failure should not eject the user from the task. It should remain another state of the original order.

The first product work already needed processing, success, and failure states. The fuller recovery model, however, remained a later design direction and should not be presented as a completely shipped first-release capability. In that direction, failure preserved as much of the known order context as possible: the money task, amount, receiving account, channel, order ID, current state, and reason. The next action then depended on what actually failed. An expired quote could refresh. An account issue could return to editing. An unavailable channel could lead to another available route. A processing order could remain traceable. Only cases that could not be self-recovered should move to support — with the same order context attached. Trust came from state continuity, not from more reassuring error copy.

BOUNDARY

Do not claim that funds are safe or arrival is guaranteed. Do not present the complete recovery center as a fully shipped first-release feature.

BOUNDED CLOSE

The immediate work had a clear boundary. The larger money architecture remained a research-guided product direction.

The public value of this case comes from two layers. The first is concrete VND / VietQR product work: sell / receive semantics, receiving account, 60-second quote, confirmation, visible order states, and interaction work around result and failure. The second is the later On/Off Ramp research and direction: moving from surface competitor comparison to transaction relationships and responsibility fit, then returning that diagnosis to money-intent entry, channel decision information, preview, order state, and recovery structure. The latter was not a fully shipped On/Off Ramp replacement. It more accurately shows how design influenced product discussion, and how a bounded local task became a real trigger for a larger product judgment.
