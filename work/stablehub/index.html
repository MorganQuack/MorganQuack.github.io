DECISION PRESSURE

The page was becoming a container for decisions that still belonged to different functions.

Product rules, business goals, technical conditions, user and reward states, copy tense, route logic, version handling, and release scope were entering the same surface at different levels of certainty. Moving directly into high fidelity would do more than create rework: polish could make unresolved choices look settled. The open questions therefore had to return to visible product consequences. Where does this CTA go? Who is eligible? What happens when a pool is exhausted? Can the system reliably judge this condition? What does a low-version user see? Where should a rule be explained?

CURRENT VERSION

Low fidelity was not a step before the design. It was how the project regained a current version.

I used rough flows, quick visual drafts, and state splits to move the discussion out of abstract PRD language and into specific product questions: where an action went, who was eligible, when a message appeared, what the system could reliably judge, what changed by version, and which questions this release should deliberately leave unresolved. The value was not “using low fidelity.” Product, business, engineering, and design could now review the same operable product artifact, switch its states, and attach disagreement to exact product consequences. The conversation changed from which idea sounded better to what this user would actually see and where the system could reliably send them.

The value was one shared, operable product artifact with disagreement attached to exact product consequences.

DECISION CLOSURE

Once the open questions were visible, the team needed to decide which ones had to close now.

I proposed a Must / Should / Could / Won't view that connected open questions to interface consequences, risks, dependencies, and current-release treatment. It was not a way for design to replace product prioritization. It made visible what would invalidate the current experience if unresolved, what would materially reduce understanding, what could wait until the structure was stable, and what should explicitly remain outside this release. Must centered on user-state splits, primary action structure, conditions the system could reliably judge, key reward copy tense, and access to core rules. Should covered supporting explanation, Q&A / rules / terms, multilingual closure, and necessary detail. Could was where richer campaign visual treatment, motion, and optional atmosphere belonged. Won't protected the release from additional routes, states, or growth mechanics that would pull it out of shape. The responsible functions still owned the final product, business, engineering, and release decisions.

CAMPAIGN STATES

The same campaign was not the same product surface for every user.

A guest needed the opportunity and a way in. A new user needed eligibility, activation, 0 / 1000 progress, and a next action. An existing holder needed current holdings, reward logic, and a useful next step. Not Eligible, Sold Out, Finished, Reward Expired, and Benefit Activated described different realities, so action, label, visibility, progress, explanation, and route could not collapse into one generic “ended” state. The historical four-state backbone anchored the product; more detailed states were used to check implementation completeness.

The portfolio state switcher demonstrates real product-state differences; it is not a shipped end-user control.

DEPTH RELEASE

Rules needed depth without crushing the first action.

APR / APY, holding calculations, reward pools, campaign periods, yesterday's earnings, cumulative earnings, Q&A, terms, and reward history did not belong at the same visual depth. The surface kept the opportunity, current state, and next action clear; users who needed more confidence could move through hints, details, expandable rules, full terms, and history. The goal was not to hide financial conditions, but to place explanation at the moment it became useful.

03 / 04

Align user intent with routes the product can actually support

INTENT BEFORE SYSTEM ROUTE
EARN ROUTING

The Buy / Deposit debate was really about whether the user or the system should make the first routing decision.

A user seeing a reward opportunity was first thinking “I want to participate,” not whether they should Buy, Deposit, or Convert. Exposing those routes directly asked the user to understand the system path first. An Earn-led entry moved the next-route decision behind the intent, where the product could judge balance, asset, identity, eligibility, version, and campaign state. Earn created a cleaner mental model only when those judgments were reliable. The historical work also included a first-release compromise: keeping more explicit Buy / Deposit-style routes reduced routing complexity and made delivery more controllable. There is no claim here that the “ideal” CTA was automatically superior. The design trade-off was how much judgment the current product could reliably move from the user into the system.

Earn is a conditional direction with a visible first-release compromise, not a confirmed single final shipped CTA.

PRODUCT ECOLOGY

The campaign had to survive App, Web, H5, eligibility, and version state to become real product work.

The StableHub campaign did ship across multiple surfaces, but those surfaces did not have identical roles. App handled campaign entry, eligibility / KYC, low-version behavior, and continued use. Web connected the fuller campaign experience to trading, deposit, and conversion. H5 supported lighter viewing, invitation, and sharing. The case does not claim that design independently owned a complete three-surface system. It shows that the same campaign states, rules, and user actions had to survive real product constraints such as eligibility, version handling, handoff, and sharing.

04 / 04

Make the launch reviewable and keep the outcome bounded

PAGE STRUCTURE -> LEARNING
REVIEWABLE BEHAVIOR

A growth page should help the team learn where the user's decision happened.

Total asset growth could show that an outcome happened, but not why users acted. Page structure and tracking needed to map back to real questions: did users notice the opportunity, understand eligibility, respond to a reward card, choose a route, hesitate around rules, use invitation or sharing, and return to follow holdings and rewards? Tracking was therefore not a separate analytics product. It was reviewability attached to real page modules: page module → user question → behavior signal → business / product question.

RESULT / CONTRIBUTION

The business result is worth showing. It cannot replace the design story.

The currently supportable records provide two public outcome signals: retail-side stablecoin holdings on OSL Global passed 10 million USD for the first time, and about 70% of one recorded StableHub-related increase came from RLUSD and USDGO. These signals do not prove that design alone caused the growth. They support a narrower conclusion: the campaign became a launchable product experience with clearer user states, next actions, explanation depth, and cross-surface entry, while the page structure also gave the team a way to review behavior after release.
